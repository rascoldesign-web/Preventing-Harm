In 2003 there were 980 deaths due to overdose in Michigan.
From 2004 onward, the opioid epidemic that began in 1999 amplified, leading to more deaths across the state.
Deaths continued to climb throughout the years, as opioids were overprescribed and underregulated.
These deaths weren't unnoticed as regulators made moves to solve symptoms of the problem rather than the cause.
Regulations were soon placed on opioid prescriptions; however, this didn't solve the addictions that people were dependent on. Users shifted to heroin, a cheaper, stronger, more dangerous alternative.
Across the country, national naloxone policies began to expand, allowing naloxone, an overdose-reversing drug, to become more accessible, but this was in its earliest stages.
Efforts like these that work to reduce the effects of drug abuse without necessitating full abstinence are called "Harm Prevention."
Fentanyl, a powerful synthetic opioid painkiller, is increasingly found mixed into heroin, and users had no way of knowing as they became addicted to an even harder substance.
By 2016, fentanyl had become the leading driver of overdose deaths in the United States, overtaking heroin and accelerating mortality.
However, this wasn't the only thing that happened. Statewide naloxone standing orders were passed in Michigan, allowing naloxone distribution and accessibility to grow and expand, saving more lives.
Michigan overdose deaths continued rising, driven almost entirely by fentanyl.
Michigan's Good Samaritan Law passes, granting legal protection from arrest or prosecution when calling for help during an overdose.
Syringe service programs, which provide users with clean syringes and treatment referrals, begin.
For nearly the first time, overdose deaths decline, showing the effects of harm prevention.
With the emergence of COVID-19, treatment programs, recovery support, and harm reduction services statewide were disrupted. Isolation, economic stress, and reduced access to care drove overdose deaths back up.
From this disruption, overdoses spiked at a higher rate than in any other year.
Overdose deaths reach their peak across Michigan at 31 deaths per 100,000.
Throughout the US, the highest recorded deaths were reached, with over 80,000 opioid deaths alone.
Even with these deaths, many more were prevented as overdose reversals reached new highs.
As infrastructure began to reopen, deaths decreased. Harm prevention methods expanded and helped reduce overdoses, supporting communities as they began to recover.
2024 marks the third consecutive year of decline. In a few years, Michigan's overdose death rate has dropped from one of the highest in the nation to below the national average.

Michigan's progress reflects a broader pattern.
West Virginia, and many states like it, have faced some of the highest overdose death rates with more limited harm prevention access.
States like Massachusetts have seen declining deaths through expanded harm prevention efforts.
The difference is measurable: where harm prevention is stronger, fewer people die.
In the end, it's up to us as a community to support harm prevention by backing local programs, advocating for expanded treatment access, and challenging stigma around drug use.
